Storage devices & media

Every storage decision a computer designer makes is the same trade: fast and tiny and expensive, or slow and huge and cheap. You cannot have both. Once you can feel how enormous that gap really is, the whole storage hierarchy stops being a list to memorise and starts being obvious.

What actually counts as memory

The word "memory" gets used loosely. In this unit it means anything that holds bits — and each kind differs on two questions: does it survive a power cut, and can you write to it?

The naming pattern is the shortcut. PROM, EPROM, EEPROM — each letter added on the front tells you what new thing you can do. P = you can program it once. EP = erasable, with ultraviolet light. EEP = erasable electrically, so it can be done in place without pulling the chip out. Flash is just EEPROM that erases in large blocks instead of byte by byte, which is what makes it fast enough for a whole drive.

Volatile or not?

Volatile means the contents vanish when the power goes. This is the distinction examiners ask about most, and it is the one students most often get backwards. Drag each item into the right bin.

The storage hierarchy

Six levels, ordered by one thing: how long the processor has to wait. Everything else — price, capacity, whether it survives a power cut — follows from that position. Click a level.

Read it as one sentence. As you go down the hierarchy, access time gets longer, capacity gets larger, and cost per byte gets smaller. Those three move together, always, in every machine ever built. If an exam question gives you one of them, you can infer the other two.

Three ways to find something

Two devices can hold the same file and still take wildly different times to produce it, because they search differently. Pick a track number and watch each medium go looking for it.

The exam distinction. Serial or sequential access means passing over everything in front of the target — magnetic tape has no choice. Direct access means jumping close to the target, then reading a short way — hard disks and optical discs move a head to the right track. Random access means every location costs the same, no matter where it is — RAM and solid state. Note the trap: a hard disk is often loosely called "random access", but strictly it is direct, because where the head is right now still affects the time.

Counting capacity

A bit is one switch. Eight of them make a byte, which is enough to hold one character. Everything after that is just multiplying by 1024 and giving it a new name.

What actually fits?

Capacity questions in exams are always the same shape: total size divided by item size. Try it.

The 1000 versus 1024 trap. Manufacturers sell a "500 GB" drive using 1 GB = 1,000,000,000 bytes. Your operating system counts in 1024s and reports about 465 GB. Nothing was stolen — the two are counting in different bases. For this unit, use 1 KB = 1024 bytes unless a question says otherwise.

Input, output or storage?

Some devices genuinely belong in two places, and those are the ones worth arguing about. Sort what you can, then read the note underneath.

Input

Sends data into the computer

Output

Presents data to the outside world

Storage

Holds data for later
The ones that break the rule. A touchscreen is input and output in one panel. A USB drive both receives and supplies data, but we classify it as storage because holding data is its purpose. A modem sends and receives. When a question forces one answer, ask what the device is for, not what it is capable of.
